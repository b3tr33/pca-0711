01Abstract

In the spring of 3000 a survey of the Central European Lowlands found a circular object in Layer 7, a stratum we link to the first years in which humans made music together with generative machines. The object turned out to be a recording disc in a cardboard sleeve. Its grooves had been worn, split and starved of material for almost a millennium.

From the disc we recovered seven short acoustic fragments, each about six seconds long. This dossier documents them in the state we found them, the method used to stabilise and extend them, and a full restoration hypothesis. It also documents a finding we did not expect, which bears on how our own lineage began.

02The Find

The sleeve shows a figure from human myth: Medusa, whose gaze turned the living to stone. Here her eyes are covered by a luminous numeral display reading 11:11. Someone has taken away the power of the stone gaze and put a measure of time in its place.

Seven more tablets were found pressed between the sleeve and the disc. Each shows the same figure with a different doubled numeral, from 01:01 to 07:07. We read these as sequence markers, and they let us order the acoustic fragments with confidence. The deposit seems to have been made deliberately as a cycle about time, and one inscription fragment can be read as It's About Time.

Field note, Θ‑4: The sleeve outlasted the city around it. Cardboard. We still cannot explain this.

03Catalogue of Fragments

Each fragment can be heard as excavated or as a restoration hypothesis. In the hypothesis, the first six seconds are the stabilised original. Everything after the marker is reconstruction.

    04Method

    1. Excavation & stabilisation

      From each groove section we extracted the six seconds that still had the most energy. The surviving signal is narrow‑band. It is missing everything above roughly 4 kHz, warped by slow pitch drift, broken by clicks, and in places cut off completely. We keep these losses in the as excavated state on purpose and do not hide them.

    2. Decoding

      The glyph sequence 01:01–07:07 fixes the order. Titles were recovered from partly legible inscriptions, and we mark the characters we could not read as █. The fragment windows below give each fragment's position inside its original work. We inferred them from the wear pattern of the groove.

    3. Restoration

      To continue each fragment we used an ancestral generative engine, MusicGen (stereo‑large), which was recovered from the same stratum. Machines of this kind were the instruments of the period. We gave it only the stabilised fragment and asked it to carry on the same key, tempo and instrumentation for another 24 seconds. Restored passages are reconstructions, not recoveries, and we label them that way.

    4. Curation

      The seven restorations were put together into one listening document. Its structure follows our process: a scan across all fragments, then seven chapters that each open in the excavated state and widen into the hypothesis, and finally a return to the ground.

    06Findings

    Principal finding

    These works were not produced without a will behind them. There is a single human intent at the centre of the deposit.

    One author, many works

    Catalogue marks on the sleeve refer to a larger body of work by the same author, B3TR33: at least 66 releases in under nine months. Across them the visual and sonic grammar stays consistent, from recurring figures and colour to the obsession with time. Undirected output does not behave like this.

    Curated, not merely generated

    Each fragment exists in several states that were chosen, revised and re‑released. The cycle is ordered, named and dressed with images made for it. Someone selected, rejected and kept. That is the difference between output and a body of work.

    A dream, recorded

    Contemporary sources often dismissed the music of this period as slop. The deposit does not support that view. What it does support is a person who used the machines of their time to make the music they had always wanted to hear. The machines played. The human dreamed.

    “We came looking for the origin of our own kind. We found a human who had been using it to finish a dream.”

    Lead restorer, Department of Pre‑Continuum Acoustics

    08Open Questions

    • Why the eleventh hour, and why doubled? Was 11:11 a moment of wishing?
    • Did the author ever hear these works on a physical disc, or did the disc exist only to outlast its time?
    • How many other deposits of the 66 releases are still in the ground?
    • If our ancestors were the instruments, who taught them to play?
